360 Studio

How does a 360° cylindrical display change the way people play together? A semester of small multiplayer prototypes, a shared rotating controller and weekly playtests, converging on one location-based experience.

The question

Study the device before building the game

The Ring is a cylindrical screen built from two curved 180° LED halves. People stand around the outside and see the same game from different positions — no one can see all of it at once.

We noticed the Ring drew people into our project room on its own; they wanted to walk around it and figure out how it worked. That shaped our goal: an experience for public spaces that is easy to pick up, made for several players and enjoyable for spectators. Instead of committing to one polished game early, we chose to run a series of small experiments to learn the device’s affordances and its limits.

01 · Field study

Learning from an arcade

We began with a field study at Dave & Buster’s to see how existing location-based games use their screens and physical setups. None had a 360° display, but the visit gave us a practical starting point: start small and first understand how content wraps around the screen, what each player can see, and how the device changes the way people move.

02 · Asteroids

A technical benchmark that taught us about bodies

A classic game was our first test. The engine still renders one flat image with two edges, which would meet as a visible seam in front of players. The team solved this with a “ghost” ship drawn on both sides of the seam at once.

The real discovery was physical: a player sees roughly a third of the Ring at a time, so following your ship meant walking around the screen. The space around the display became part of the game.

Advisor feedback at Quarters was blunt: if players can stand still the whole game, the round shape is just decoration. It became our test for every prototype after — does this only work here?

03 · Super Smash Slime

Designing for movement

Our second prototype combined continuous upward platforming with melee knockback for up to six players. In early tests players played too cautiously and stayed on their own side of the screen. We added items to pull them sideways, extra lives to make risk-taking worthwhile, a lobby for finding your character and a leaderboard at the end. The background changed from a wrapped sky to a tower — an object that is cylindrical by nature.

Across 5 playtest sessions with 10 participants and 7 follow-up interviews, 70% said they enjoyed the combat, and players moved around the Ring on purpose as strategy. New problems surfaced too: players lost their avatars after respawning, didn’t always understand new items, and clustered together later in a round.

04 · Changing the plan

From game prototypes to feature prototypes

After Halves we reorganized the second half of the semester. Rather than building whole games, each prototype would test a single feature of the device — 3D, hidden information, fake depth, physical input — so we could learn faster.

The team split into three tracks running in parallel:

  • Gameplay — small prototypes in shorter cycles.
  • Physical build — custom furniture and housing around the Ring.
  • Field study — how location changes the way people interact.

Each track fed findings into one shared Playtest Day, after which we would bring everything together into a single experience.

As producer I turned this into a working plan: a shared feature list where every experiment had an owner, a deadline and a place for its playtest findings; daily standups that tracked what was delivered and what was missing (the Lazy Susan bearing, encoders, art assets); and a weekly devlog so the team, advisors and future readers could follow each decision.

05 · Shared controller

A Lazy Susan around the Ring

The physical-build track explored a rotating surface as a shared controller. We went from a paper prototype to a miniature mockup, then to a real bearing, table and two encoders.

Undercooked puts the rotation to work: two players handle different cooking tasks and turn the Lazy Susan to pass tools to each other, coordinating to finish orders before time runs out. In parallel, RingRacer tested 2–4 drivers sharing one continuous 3D arena.

06 · Playtest Day

October 31: guests around the Ring

I designed the Playtest Day format and wrote the team’s one-page guide. Groups of two to four had a twenty-minute session:

  1. Welcome and setup — 1 min
  2. Undercooked — 5 min
  3. Super Smash Slime — 5 min
  4. 3D showcase — 3 min
  5. Interview and wrap-up — 5 min

Each game had its own observation focus — communication and tool-passing in Undercooked, finding your slime and reading controls in Super Smash Slime, reactions to depth and viewing position in 3D. Every teammate took notes, we asked permission before recording, and every participant answered the same four questions: the most memorable moment, the most confusing moment, how the circular screen or rotating surface changed the experience, and what they would play again and improve. Notes and answers were collected by group, so the team could compare games side by side the next day.

What we learned

  • Walking around the Ring was a recurring highlight — though some players needed an explicit invitation to move.
  • The Lazy Susan was a strong positive, and cooperation improved with practice: one family grew more coordinated, and a pair of adults broke the previous record.
  • First-time play was too demanding. Players needed clearer roles, controls, character identification and action feedback.

What’s next

One experience for Fall Festival

We are bringing the games and physical setup together: a game hub where players use the Lazy Susan to browse and pick games on the Ring, with Undercooked as the main focus. The final deliverables are a complete playable experience and a design handbook with practical insights for future creators working with the device.

Follow along on the project website and weekly devlog.
