Overview

A screen no one can see all at once.

The Ring is a cylinder built from two curved 180° LED panels. Players stand around it, each seeing a different part of one continuous world. Our team’s job: find out what this device is good for, and build a polished multiplayer experience for public spaces around it.

01 · Understanding the device

Explore the platform before choosing a game.

In week one we assembled the display and tested ordinary video on it. Two properties stood out: no single viewer can see the whole image, and players can approach from every side. We decided not to port existing games, but to design around those two facts.

02 · Field research

What makes arcade games instantly playable?

I organized a field study at Dave & Buster’s. The games that worked were quick to understand, needed no instructions, used approachable controls, created spectacle and made losing cheap. We turned this into five design constraints for every prototype:

03 · Prototype as a research tool

Asteroids: the device changes how bodies move.

Our first playable test wrapped a classic game around the cylinder. Players ran, collided and blocked each other to follow their ships — movement became part of play. Advisors were blunt: it still felt like a flat game wrapped around a cylinder. We distilled their critique into nine recurring patterns; one, hidden information and movement, came up in every conversation.

How might we design play that only works around a 360° screen, and that strangers can pick up in seconds?

05 · A producer’s pivot

From game prototypes to feature prototypes.

At Halves, faculty asked the question we couldn’t yet answer: why a continuous cylinder, and not several flat screens in a circle? Whole games were taking too long to teach us that. I restructured the second half of the semester into single-feature experiments and three parallel workstreams, each with owners, deadlines and a findings log.

Gameplay

Short prototype cycles

One device property per experiment: 3D depth, hidden information, physical input.

06 · Physical build

A Lazy Susan around the Ring.

The physical track sketched several installations and chose a rotating ring beneath the fixed display. Turning it moves tools between player stations — a shared controller that forces conversation. We went from concept art to a paper prototype, a miniature mockup and a working table with encoders.

07 · 3D exploration

Can one screen hold depth for many viewers?

Viewpoint-based cameras only looked right within about ±30° of a viewer; between viewpoints, images doubled. RingRacer took another route — a central 360° capture mapped onto the cylinder, with motion parallax to pull players “into” the screen.

08 · Playtest Day

Twelve groups, one shared format.

I designed the session and wrote the team’s guide: 20 minutes per group — three games, a short interview, the same four questions for everyone, an observation focus per game. Very different guests — families, ETC alumni, first-time gamers — produced findings we could compare side by side.

Both were true. We kept the cooperative core, and made the circular layout essential before adding stations.

What’s next

One experience, and a handbook.

For Fall Festival: a Lazy Susan game hub on the Ring with Undercooked at its center. Alongside it, a design handbook so future teams start where we ended. Follow the weekly devlog.

Reflection

Producing research means designing how a team learns.

The most valuable thing I built wasn’t a feature — it was the loop: a clear question per prototype, a consistent playtest format and a record of every decision. It turned twelve very different groups into evidence the whole team could act on.
