Overview

A screen you have to walk around

The Ring is a cylindrical display made of two curved 180° LED screens. Players stand around the outside and see the same game from different positions — no one can see all of it at once. Our six-person team is exploring how this device changes what players see, how they move and how they play together, with the goal of a polished multiplayer experience for public spaces and a design handbook for future creators.

As the team’s producer, I own how we plan, learn and communicate: the schedule, the playtests, the research and the record of every decision.

Current direction

Play in the round, with a table everyone shares

After four prototypes and two rounds of public playtesting, the experience is converging on three ideas:

01

A shared physical controller

A rotating Lazy Susan around the Ring. In Undercooked, two cooks pass tools by turning it, so every rotation is a negotiation.

02

Movement as part of play

Players walk around the screen to follow the action. The space around the display becomes part of the game.

03

A game hub for Fall Festival

Guests use the Lazy Susan to browse and pick games directly on the Ring, joining everything into one experience.

Problem

A novel screen is not a reason to play

Our first prototype worked technically, but advisors pointed out that it felt like a flat game wrapped around a cylinder. If players can stand still the whole time, the round shape is only decoration. The challenge became designing play that needs this device — and that strangers in a public space can understand in seconds.

HOW MIGHT WEdesign multiplayer play that only works around a 360° screen, is easy to pick up in a public space, and is fun for players and spectators alike?

Research

Learning from arcades and advisors

I started the project with a field study at Dave & Buster’s to see how location-based games use screens and physical setups, and organized advisor critiques into recurring patterns the team could act on.

Field study

Start small, learn the device first

No arcade had a 360° screen, so we chose to understand wrapping, visibility and movement before designing a full game.

Advisor critique

The “only works here” test

If a game can be played standing still, the shape is decoration. Every later prototype had to pass this test.

Early playtests

Bodies are part of the interface

Players can see about a third of the Ring at once, so following your character means walking — and bumping into each other became part of the fun.

Process

Four prototypes, one question each

Prototype 1

360 Asteroids — a technical benchmark

A classic game across the cylinder. The team solved the visible seam where the image’s two edges meet with a “ghost” ship drawn on both sides at once.

Takeaway

Players walked around the screen to follow their ships. Movement was the most interesting thing the device created.

Prototype 2

Super Smash Slime — designing for movement

Up to six players on platforms climbing a tower. Early players hid on their own side, so we added items, extra lives, a lobby and a leaderboard to push them to move and take risks.

Takeaway

Across 5 sessions with 10 players, 70% enjoyed the combat — but players lost their avatars after respawning and clustered later in a round.

Changing the plan at Halves

Whole games were taking too long to teach us anything. I reorganized the second half of the semester into feature prototypes — each one testing a single property such as 3D depth, hidden information or physical input — and three parallel tracks: gameplay, physical build and field study. To keep them moving, I set up a shared feature list with an owner, deadline and findings column for every experiment, daily standups tracking what was delivered or missing, and a weekly devlog.

Prototype 3

Undercooked + Lazy Susan — a shared controller

Two cooks with different tasks pass tools by rotating a real Lazy Susan. We went from a paper prototype to a miniature mockup to a working table with encoders.

Takeaway

Rotation creates real negotiation — who needs the knife, who turns the table.

Prototype 4

RingRacer — a continuous 3D world

Two to four drivers share one 3D arena that wraps around the screen.

Takeaway

Players loved the seamless world, but steering, depth and viewpoint together created a high mental load.

Playtest Day

Designing a playtest that teaches the team

For our October 31 Playtest Day I designed the session format and wrote the team’s one-page guide. Each group of two to four guests had twenty minutes: a one-minute welcome, five minutes each of Undercooked and Super Smash Slime, a three-minute 3D showcase and a five-minute interview. Each game had its own observation focus, every teammate took notes, we asked permission before recording, and every participant answered the same four questions. Notes were collected by group, so the next day we could compare games side by side.

Strongest positive

Walking around the Ring

Every morning group mentioned enjoying moving around the screen — though some needed an invitation to discover it.

Highlight

The Lazy Susan

Five of the six morning groups said they liked rotating it, and cooperation grew with practice; one pair broke the previous record.

Biggest gap

First-time clarity

Players struggled to find their character, read roles and see the effect of their actions.

“It was really rewarding figuring out how to optimize speed.”— Playtest Day guest, Undercooked
“Two monitors would have been enough.”— Playtest Day guest, on the same game

Holding both comments side by side shaped the team’s next decision: keep the cooperative core, but make the circular layout essential to play before adding more stations or players.

What’s next

One experience for Fall Festival

We are merging the games and physical build into a single experience: a Lazy Susan game hub on the Ring, with Undercooked as the main focus — a practice round to make roles clear, clearer feedback for every action, and a two-player baseline to measure any expansion against. Follow along on the project website and weekly devlog.

Reflection

  1. A new device is a question, not an answer

    The hardest and most useful critique was that a cylindrical screen alone is not a reason to play. Asking “does this only work here?” of every idea kept us honest.

  2. Fun and clarity are separate measurements

    Guests could love a game and still not know which character was theirs. Separating “did they enjoy it” from “did they understand it” made our findings far more actionable.

  3. Producing research means designing how the team learns

    A consistent playtest format, shared observation focus and organized notes turned twelve very different groups into evidence the whole team could use.
