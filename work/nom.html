Overview

Yelp, reimagined for students

NOM is a redesign of Yelp for college students, created in UCSD Design Co’s UX project studio — a pre-professional program that pairs student designers with an industry-style process.

Problem

Too many options, not enough decision

Surveys and early usability sessions showed a clear pattern: faced with long lists, students got frustrated or abandoned the search entirely. And they care about very different things — price, atmosphere, how a place looks, how fast they can decide.

HOW MIGHT WEhelp college students decide where to eat quickly, without losing what makes the choice personal?

Research

Four kinds of student eaters

We synthesized findings in an affinity diagram and audited Yelp, Google reviews, Tripadvisor, the Michelin Guide, Uber Eats, DoorDash and Postmates on how easy their filters and navigation were to use. Personas showed why one ranked list fails:

Persona

The campus foodie

No car, loves trying new cuisines, spends on drinks and desserts.

Persona

The picky commuter

Cooks often; eating out is rare, so it has to be worth it.

Persona

The indecisive reviewer

Reads everything, still can’t choose — and gets bored of the usual options.

Persona

The social eater

Cares more about atmosphere and friends than the food itself.

Storyboards put them in real moments: a late drive home through an unfamiliar town, a sunny beach day on a budget, a new student overwhelmed by choices while carrying groceries without a car.

Process

Borrowing the swipe — then refining it

Round 1

Swipe to decide

I researched how social apps use swiping to keep people engaged and prototyped a recommendation flow that narrows choices one card at a time.

Takeaway

Swiping alone wasn’t enough: some students wanted quick, budget-friendly picks, others cared most about how a place looked.

Round 2

Giving users control over “a good match”

I added aesthetic filters alongside price, so students could shape their recommendations without bringing back the long list.

Reflection

  1. Turn messy feedback into a few decisions

    Conflicting preferences became two clear levers — price and aesthetics — instead of a pile of features.

  2. Familiar patterns lower the learning curve

    Borrowing the swipe from social apps made a new kind of search feel instantly usable.
